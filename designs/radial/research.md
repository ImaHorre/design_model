# Radial Step Emulsification for Physics, Modeling, and Manufacturability

## Framing the architecture shift

Your current ladder-style device is a **mixed-boundary hydraulic network**: a dead-end oil rail feeds a through-flow water rail through repeated rungs, so the local driving pressure across each rung varies along the device, and reverse invasion can occur where the local oil pressure falls below the local water pressure. Your earlier memo also framed droplet formation in that platform as a pressure-sensitive confined-displacement stage followed by a more capillary-controlled bulb growth and necking stage. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file0

The radial concept in `design_notes.md` changes the **global boundary conditions** much more than it changes the **local step-emulsification physics**. In the proposed wheel, oil is supplied from a hub into many nominally identical radial DFUs, each terminating at the rim in an open continuous-phase bath while preserving the local V5-30 rim geometry at 30 × 10 μm with 60 μm pitch. In the idealized limit, each DFU has the same upstream pressure and nearly the same outlet pressure, so the array stops looking like a ladder network and starts looking like a repeated single-nozzle problem with a shared hub and shared bath. fileciteturn0file2

That means the radial idea is actually **closer to classic dead-end microchannel emulsification plates** than to your present ladder architecture. In the microchannel-emulsification literature, grooved or straight-through arrays supplied through a central feed and discharging into a continuous phase have long been used in dead-end mode, and large silicon chips on the order of **12,000 microchannels** have been reported while still producing droplet size that is nearly independent of dispersed-phase flow below a critical value. That is encouraging for your proposed scale, because your note’s maximum of roughly **6,649 DFUs** is in the same order of magnitude, not a leap into a completely uncharted regime. citeturn15view1turn15view0 fileciteturn0file2

So the correct mental model is not “a completely different droplet generator.” It is “the same **local** step-emulsification family, but with a **very different global hydraulic and operational bottleneck**.” In the ladder design, the bottleneck is pressure distribution along two coupled manifolds. In the radial design, the bottleneck shifts toward **hub feed losses, outlet crowding/clearance, wetting stability, and fabrication scatter**. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file2 citeturn15view4turn27view0

The most useful way to read `design_notes.md` is not as “wrong” or “right,” but as a set of **good first-order statements that need a sharper boundary on where they stop being true**. That is exactly where the literature helps. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn10view3turn10view2turn9view4

## What the droplet physics at the rim is likely doing

At the rim, the radial design is still living inside the standard **step-emulsification / spontaneous microchannel-emulsification** physics. The oldest and still very useful picture is the **quasi-static curvature-balance** picture: as a confined tongue exits into a deeper region, the downstream bulb expands, its curvature changes, and a point is reached where a static curvature balance can no longer be maintained. That loss of balance is what makes breakup geometrically robust and what explains why droplet size is often much less sensitive to flow rate than in shear-driven T-junctions or flow-focusers. citeturn0search0turn11search15

A more dynamic picture, now well supported by simulation, adds an important detail that matters for your modeling: breakup is not only “the drop grows until it pinches.” It can involve an **adverse pressure gradient** and **backflow of the continuous phase into the nozzle region**, which helps create the neck and trigger rupture. The same simulation work also explains why step emulsification fails below a critical nozzle aspect ratio: when the nozzle is too squat, the interface can transition smoothly from the confined geometry into the reservoir without the dynamical rearrangements needed for pinching. The reported breakup threshold is around **\(w/h \approx 2.6\)**. citeturn0search2turn10view2

That threshold is especially important for your geometry because your preserved outlet section is **30 μm wide and 10 μm deep**, i.e. **\(w/h = 3\)**. That is on the safe side, but it is **not a huge safety margin**. It is only modestly above the literature threshold. In practice, that means “small” fabrication or compliance effects are not small. A slight effective increase in height, a slight local narrowing, corner rounding, or a compliant roof can push the effective aspect ratio toward the critical band where breakup becomes less robust. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn10view2turn9view6

The next important local timescale is the **necking time**. This is where the literature most strongly corrects the simplified radial note. Mittal and co-workers found that the characteristic droplet-formation timescale in step emulsification is governed primarily by the **outer-phase viscosity-to-interfacial-tension ratio**, with a smaller viscosity-ratio correction, and that this timescale sets a **maximum production rate of small droplets**. They also found that in arrays with crossflow, a droplet boundary layer can develop; in the small-drop regime the local dynamics remain relatively independent of that crossflow, but in the large-drop regime drop–drop collisions can trigger fragmentation and broaden the size distribution. citeturn10view3turn9view3

This matters because it means your radial DFU period is better thought of as something like

\[
T_{\text{cycle}} \approx t_{\text{reset}} + t_{\text{neck}} + t_{\text{clear}},
\]

not simply “hydraulic refill + capillary growth,” with the understanding that \(t_{\text{reset}}\) can itself hide more than one mechanism. In a clean open bath, \(t_{\text{reset}}\) may indeed be just the local capillary reset of a short water-filled plug near the step. But in related spontaneous devices, the same phase of the cycle can be controlled by **dynamic interfacial tension**, where the meniscus waits until surfactant adsorption lowers \(\gamma(t)\) enough for the force balance to permit the next motion. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn9view4turn8view1

Your existing deep-research memo already pointed at precisely these alternative mechanisms — backflow, dynamic interfacial tension, and collective effects — and the radial architecture does not make them go away. It just changes which one is most likely to dominate. If your present SDS system is clean, strongly surface-active, and run at moderate per-DFU frequency, the short local reset picture is plausible. If you later reduce surfactant, change oils, change surface treatment, or push throughput harder, the adsorption-lag and backflow pictures have to come back into the reduced-order model. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file2 citeturn9view4turn0search2

One more caution is very specific to your fluid choice direction. In related EDGE experiments, **sunflower oil with SDS** performed poorly on glass because interactions at the solid surface changed the effective wetting behavior; stable monodisperse operation was recovered when the surface was protected by an irreversibly adsorbed hydrophilic protein layer. That does not prove your exact system will fail, but it is a strong warning that **sunflower oil + SDS + substrate chemistry** should be treated as a first-order variable, not as a nuisance parameter. citeturn9view0turn9view1

## What will actually limit throughput and uniformity at the device level

The **big win** of the radial layout is that it removes the ladder’s built-in spatial pressure gradient between oil and water rails. In the ladder, local driving pressure varies position-by-position, and reverse invasion is a natural failure mode. In the radial design, the first useful reduced model is much simpler:

\[
\Delta P_{\text{DFU}} \approx P_{\text{hub}} - P_{\text{bath}} - \Delta P_{\text{cap}} - \Delta P_{\text{dyn}},
\]

where \(\Delta P_{\text{dyn}}\) is a placeholder for whatever local correction you use for necking, backflow, and dynamic-\(\gamma\) effects. That is already a much more favorable modeling starting point than the current mixed-boundary ladder solver. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file2 citeturn10view3turn9view4

But the radial design is only “simple” if the **hub is truly a good pressure equalizer** and the **bath is truly a benign sink**. Your note already catches the first point: because channels overlap near the center, a hub plenum is geometrically unavoidable, and if that plenum is shallow and fed through a single small central inlet, it can consume a large fraction of the available pressure budget. That means the key optimization is not really the array radius by itself; it is **how the hub is fed**. A deeper hub, a larger inlet, or ideally a ring-fed plenum at the channel-entry radius attacks the right bottleneck without touching the local rim geometry. fileciteturn0file2

The second hidden bottleneck is the **rim itself**. In a radial wheel, every droplet is born on one circumference, so the outer annulus becomes a traffic-management problem. The step-emulsification literature is very clear that nozzles are sensitive to **obstruction at the exit**; if droplets linger near the nozzles, they disturb the formation of subsequent droplets and reduce the maximum usable productivity. That is why open collecting channels, buoyancy-assisted clearance, or slit-based removal concepts have been introduced in high-throughput devices. citeturn15view4turn9view5

This is exactly where your “open bath at atmospheric pressure” assumption needs tightening. For a quiescent bath with plenty of free volume and enough droplet migration away from the rim, treating \(P_{\text{bath}}\) as approximately constant is fine. But if you add a tangential clearance flow, a narrow annular collector, or any geometry that forces droplets to remain near the rim, you reintroduce **bath-side pressure gradients and collective interactions**. In the small-drop regime that can still be manageable and sometimes even helpful, but in the larger-drop or higher-throughput regime it becomes a real coupling path. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn10view3turn8view1

A third device-level limiter is **flow variation created by fabrication defects or partial clogging**, not by the idealized symmetry. Recent parallel step-emulsification work shows that even when droplet size is robust to moderate flow variation, some nozzles can still be forced into jetting if local flow exceeds a threshold, and accidental clogging is still observed in otherwise well-designed arrays. In other words, the radial wheel makes equal flow *easier* to achieve than the ladder, but you still want individual DFU resistance to dominate over common-feed and common-outlet disturbances. citeturn27view0turn22view3

The design implication is straightforward: **upstream resistance is still valuable**, but for a more refined reason than in the ladder. In the ladder you used it to flatten a built-in manifold gradient. In the radial array you use it to **isolate each DFU from hub transients, local bath perturbations, and fabrication scatter**, while keeping per-DFU flow below the threshold where dripping gives way to jetting. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn10view3turn27view0

## Manufacturing and materials realities

The most important fabrication variable in your radial design is not simply “can I make 30 μm features?” It is **can I make the outlet height and wetting state repeatably across thousands of nozzles?** In your own rectangular-channel model the hydraulic conductance carries an \(h^3\) dependence, and the step-emulsification literature repeatedly ties droplet size to nozzle height or at least to its order of magnitude. That means height scatter hits both **flow distribution** and **drop size/regime margin** at the same time. A ±5% height error around a 10 μm target implies roughly a ±15% conductance swing before you even account for wetting changes. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn9view3turn15view3

If you prototype in **PDMS**, compliance is not a side issue. Pressure-driven experiments on flexible PDMS channels found an approximately linear relationship between pressure and deformation and reported pressure drops up to **35% lower than rigid-wall predictions** because the channel geometry changed under load. That matters a lot here because your 30 × 10 μm outlet is already only a little above the breakup threshold in aspect ratio. A compliant roof makes the hydraulic model wrong *and* erodes the emulsification regime margin at the same time. citeturn9view6turn10view2

PDMS also raises a second problem: **solvent compatibility**. Solvent/oil absorption can deform channels by swelling the elastomer. In one study, hexadecane caused measurable roof deformation of PDMS microchannels, while perfluorinated oils did not. The exact deformation will depend on your oil, geometry, and wall thickness, but the general lesson is robust: if you use PDMS, oil choice is also a geometry choice. citeturn23view2

The third PDMS issue is **surface stability**. Oxygen plasma can make PDMS hydrophilic, but that state is unstable and decays as the surface recovers hydrophobicity. More durable treatment schemes, such as plasma followed by PVA deposition, have been shown to maintain hydrophilicity for weeks and support stable oil-in-water droplet generation; the same work also warns that overly aggressive plasma treatment can damage PDMS and weaken bonding. If your target system is O/W step emulsification, this is not optional process trivia — it is directly tied to whether the walls remain water-wet enough for clean snap-off. citeturn23view3turn24view0

By contrast, **silicon/glass** gives you much better dimensional precision, rigidity, optical clarity, and chemical/thermal robustness, at the cost of more involved fabrication and historically higher chip cost. Those materials are also the established basis for large dead-end or straight-through MC plates, including multi-array single-crystal-silicon chips at scales that are comparable to or larger than your proposed DFU count. For a radial disk whose value proposition is “many identical outlets behaving the same way,” rigid substrates are much more aligned with the physics you are trying to preserve. citeturn23view0turn19search6turn15view0turn15view1

So the manufacturing conclusion is fairly crisp. If the immediate goal is **rapid learning**, PDMS is still useful because it lets you test hub concepts, rim clearance ideas, and wetting strategies quickly. If the goal is a **credible high-count throughput demonstrator**, a stiff substrate is the safer endgame because your design sits in a parameter region where small geometry drifts have outsized hydraulic and regime consequences. citeturn9view6turn23view0turn15view0

## Modeling priorities and design rules

For the radial design, I would not start by building a giant CFD model of the whole wheel. I would build a **layered model** with only the pieces that can actually change a design decision.

First, keep a **single-DFU local model** that includes a capillary entry threshold, a necking law, and a regime switch for failed breakup or jetting. The literature supports using geometry and curvature for the trigger condition, and using a necking-time closure that depends on outer viscosity, interfacial tension, and viscosity ratio. That gives you a model that is still fast enough for design sweeps but no longer assumes that one algebraic capillary number captures everything. citeturn0search0turn10view3turn10view2

Second, wrap that local law with an **axisymmetric hub/plenum model**. In the ladder design, the network solver was the main event. In the radial design, it should become a much smaller wrapper: supply pressure, hub loss, maybe compliance, then identical DFUs. This is also the right place to compare a point-fed hub, a large central hub, and a ring-fed plenum, because those choices change the global pressure budget far more than they change the local droplet physics. fileciteturn0file1 fileciteturn0file2

Third, add a **bath/rim clearance model** much earlier than intuition might suggest. In your wheel, the global limit may well be the residence time of droplets in the rim zone rather than the necking time at a nozzle. That means you need at least a reduced description of whether droplets are cleared by buoyancy, bath recirculation, or a purpose-built collector geometry. If you do not include that, you can easily over-predict the useful throughput window of the entire device. citeturn15view4turn10view3

Fourth, include **manufacturing scatter explicitly**. Because the conductance scales strongly with channel height and the regime margin is narrow in \(w/h\), a model that assumes every DFU is identical will look much better than the fabricated device ever can. For this architecture, stochastic depth, contact-angle, and partial-clogging perturbations are not “second-order realism”; they are part of the first design pass. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn27view0turn9view6

If I were turning this into immediate design rules, I would prioritize the following:

- **Protect the last part of the nozzle geometry above everything else.** Your wheel can change upstream widths, hub topology, and collector architecture, but the final outlet height, width, and step edge should remain as a tightly controlled “sacred” region because nozzle structure is what primarily controls monodispersity and regime robustness. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn26search14turn10view2  
- **Treat outlet height control as a top-level requirement, not just a fab spec.** In your geometry, a micron-scale error can materially change both conductance and effective aspect-ratio margin. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn9view6turn9view3  
- **Do not hard-code viscosity independence into the regime map.** Use viscosity-neutral channel-velocity formulas only as internal hydraulics, then apply a breakup criterion that still depends on viscosity ratio and outer-phase viscosity. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn10view3turn8view1  
- **Make droplet clearance a designed subsystem.** If the open bath is expected to carry the throughput, give it enough space and/or a collector geometry to keep the rim from becoming a congestion ring. citeturn15view4turn10view3  
- **Use direct measurements of dynamic interfacial tension and effective wetting with the actual oil/surfactant/substrate combination.** A fitted capillary back-pressure is useful, but it is not a substitute for knowing whether your surface chemistry is drifting during startup and long runs. fileciteturn0file2 citeturn9view4turn9view1turn24view0  
- **If throughput is the goal, attack the hub first.** A deeper or ring-fed plenum is a better optimization than widening the proven outlet region, because it relieves the pressure budget while preserving the local breakup physics. fileciteturn0file2

The bottom line is that the **radial architecture is a strong idea** if your goal is massive parallelization with much simpler hydraulic matching than the ladder can offer. The physics case is favorable, and the concept is aligned with proven dead-end microchannel-emulsification practice. But the design note is optimistic in three places that matter: it treats bath pressure as perfectly uniform, collapses regime limits into an over-simple capillary-number picture, and underestimates how strongly hub geometry and surface chemistry can control the real operating window. If you correct those points, the radial design stops being a speculative geometry and becomes a very credible next platform to model and fabricate. fileciteturn0file2 fileciteturn0file0 citeturn15view0turn10view3turn9view0turn23view0
